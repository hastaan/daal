# Protocol-level anatomy of circumvention in China and Iran, 2026

**Only a narrow class of tools still reliably defeats the 2026 Great Firewall and Iran's SHOMA: those that produce an authentic TLS handshake to a plausible front, avoid nested-TLS burst patterns, and can survive UDP throttling.** The rest of the ecosystem — Shadowsocks without padding, classical VMess, vanilla WireGuard, bare Hysteria, obfs4, meek on major CDNs, default Snowflake in China — has been broken, throttled, or degraded since the GFW's November 2021 fully-encrypted-traffic classifier, its October 2022 TLS-in-TLS port blocks, and Iran's protocol whitelist enforced on TCI and MCI since 2022. The practitioner gold standard is now **VLESS + XTLS-Vision + REALITY over TCP:443**, with **NaiveProxy** as a co-equal alternative on HTTP/2 and **Hysteria2 + Salamander + port-hopping** where UDP remains open. Below, each component is unpacked at the level of packet structures, handshake flows, detection signals, and the specific assumption each makes about the censor.

## How the GFW and SHOMA actually work on the wire

The GFW is a **distributed system of heterogeneous middleboxes** at the three state operator gateways (China Telecom, Unicom, Mobile), combining on-path injection with in-path dropping. Different subsystems — DNS, TLS-SNI, HTTP-Host, QUIC, fully-encrypted-traffic — maintain independent blocklists and appear to be run by different teams, evidenced by list divergence: roughly **102k domains on the TLS-SNI list, ~107k on DNS, ~105k on HTTP, and ~58k on the QUIC-SNI list** (Zohaib et al., USENIX Security 2025). Residual blocking against a triggering 3-tuple (client IP, server IP, server port) typically lasts **90–180 seconds** across subsystems.

DNS injection spoofs responses to UDP/53 queries containing a forbidden name, racing legitimate answers and often returning real third-party IPs (Facebook, Dropbox ranges) to poison caches. Encrypted DNS variants are collateral damage: **DoT/DoH to foreign resolvers are blocked via TLS-SNI**. TLS-SNI inspection requires a prior SYN plus PSH/ACK packet, after which the plaintext `server_name` field in ClientHello is read and a forged **TCP RST + RST/ACK pair** is injected to both endpoints; **any ClientHello carrying the `0xffce` ESNI extension has been dropped outright across all 65,535 ports since 29 July 2020**, with 120–180 s residual blocking — the earliest documented GFW case of deploying in-path drops at scale.

Two systems dominate modern GFW behavior. The **fully-encrypted-traffic (FET) classifier deployed 6 November 2021** (Wu et al., USENIX Security 2023) inspects the first TCP data packet and applies five exemption rules: popcount per byte outside [3.4, 4.6]; first six bytes printable ASCII; >50 % ASCII in the packet; a ≥20-byte printable run; or a positive fingerprint match against known protocols such as TLS records (`\x16\x03\x03`, `\x17\x03\x03`) or HTTP verbs. **Traffic that matches none of these exemptions is blocked**; Wu et al. estimate ~0.6 % false-positive rate on benign flows, so the GFW runs the classifier only opportunistically. This single change broke vanilla Shadowsocks (AEAD and stream), VMess, and obfs4 simultaneously, because their on-the-wire look — uniform random bytes with ~50/50 bit balance — is exactly the complement of the exemption set.

The second, newer system is **QUIC-SNI censorship active since 7 April 2024**. The GFW computes per-connection QUIC Initial keys from the destination connection ID and v1 version salt specified in RFC 9001, decrypts the Initial at wire speed, parses the inner TLS ClientHello, and if the SNI is blocklisted it **drops packets in-path** on that 3-tuple for roughly three minutes — the first UDP-layer drop-based residual at GFW scale. Weaknesses are significant: no UDP reassembly (so Chrome's default split Initials since 13 September 2024 bypass it), a port heuristic that only inspects flows where source port exceeds destination port, no support for QUIC v2, and a decryption bottleneck that degrades under load with diurnal patterns. A single spoofed Initial can also **weaponize the GFW to DoS arbitrary UDP** between external hosts; disclosed to Chinese authorities in January 2025 and only partially mitigated by March.

Active probing against Shadowsocks-like proxies has existed since **May 2019** (Alice et al., IMC 2020). When a suspicious connection is seen, probes originate from **thousands of IPs across China Telecom and Unicom** with TTL below 64 and shared TSval counters around 250 Hz, implying centralized control and roughly seven parallel probe processes. Probes include raw replay, byte-flipped replay, random-payload probes tuned to Shadowsocks IV sizes, and length-specific variants exploiting version-specific vulnerabilities. Since the 2021 passive classifier handles most detection, Xue et al. found only ~179 probes across 33,119 observed connections in 2023, but probing remains a confirmation tool.

Two additional 2025 events matter. **Wallbleed (NDSS 2025)** was a buffer over-read in the GFW's DNS Injector 3 that leaked up to 125 bytes of middlebox memory per crafted query between late 2021 and March 2024, confirming x86_64 hardware, centralized TSval clocks, and that ~99 % of recovered cross-border traffic was under 1.5 seconds old (streaming inspection). A **"wall behind a wall" (S&P 2025)** documented an independent **Henan provincial firewall active since August 2023** that has blocked ~4.2 million domains, ten times the national list at peak; it is stateless, inspects only outbound traffic, unifies HTTP-Host and TLS-SNI into a single list (unlike the national GFW), and appears aimed at economic-fraud domains after the 2022 Henan bank scandal. On **20 August 2025, between 00:34 and 01:48 Beijing time**, an unidentified device unconditionally RST-injected every TCP/443 connection across China for 74 minutes — equipment fingerprints did not match known GFW injectors.

Iran's architecture is philosophically different: it is a **National Information Network (NIN / Shabake-ye Melli-ye Ettela'at)** in which connection to the global Internet is *optional* by design, with the Telecommunication Infrastructure Company (TIC) as sole international gateway. Bock et al. (USENIX Security 2020) demonstrated — and Lange et al. (IRBlock, USENIX Security 2025) re-confirmed — that **Iran's international boundary forwards effectively only DNS, HTTP, and HTTPS**; raw OpenVPN (with its fixed 14-byte header), WireGuard (whose 4-byte type `0x01,0x00,0x00,0x00` on a 148-byte initiation packet is a perfect DPI signature), IKEv2, L2TP, SSH to non-approved hosts, and arbitrary TCP/UDP are silently dropped. International bandwidth fell from **~8 Tbps in late 2021 to ~3 Tbps in October 2022**, recovering only to ~6 Tbps by winter 2024. In February 2024 the **"Statute for Investigating Methods of Increasing Domestic Traffic Share and Countering Filter Breakers"** formalized a tiered internet: whitelisted institutions keep global access via state-licensed VPNs, while the general population is restricted to NIN. SHOMA ("intelligent filtering") layers three distinct DNS poisoners that return RFC 1918 addresses (10.10.34.34/.35/.36) — one exhibiting **TTL-reflection behavior consistent with GFW technology transfer** — plus case-sensitive HTTP-Host DPI, TLS-SNI RST after ClientHello, IPv6 blocking since May 2024, and UDP dropping that "notably impacts QUIC." The ARTICLE 19 report *Tightening the Net* (February 2026) documented **ZTE, Huawei, Tiandy, and Hikvision** equipment deployments in Iran, with the NIN architecture "increasingly mirroring features associated with China's Great Firewall."

During the **June 2025 "Twelve-Day War" with Israel**, Iran executed a "stealth blackout": BGP remained announced (no routing withdrawal), but traffic flatlined at ~3 % of normal, WhatsApp was re-blocked, and even the protocol whitelist tightened to government-approved HTTPS endpoints only. A longer blackout that began **28 February 2026 passed 53 consecutive days by 21 April**, the longest state-imposed shutdown ever recorded per NetBlocks. SpaceX waived Starlink fees inside Iran on 13 January 2026; Iran responded with L-band jamming and a 2025 law criminalizing satellite-terminal possession with door-to-door confiscations. Estimates place smuggled terminals at 50,000–100,000 in a country of 90 million.

## The TLS-in-TLS problem — the defining 2024 shift

Every tunneled HTTPS connection contains a nested TLS handshake: your browser to origin.com wrapped inside the proxy's own TLS session to the server. **Xue, Kallitsis, Houmansadr, and Ensafi (USENIX Security 2024) showed this nesting leaves a protocol-agnostic fingerprint** detectable in the first few packet-size bursts, with chi-squared and Mahalanobis tests on burst and timing sequences achieving true-positive rates above **70 % at a false-positive rate of 0.0544 %** on an ISP trace of 110 million flows over 30 days. This attack works against **Shadowsocks, VMess, Trojan, VLESS+TLS+WS, and even XTLS-Vision with its random padding**. Random padding only marginally reduces detectability because it cannot shrink the irreducible round-trip count or burst envelope of a TLS 1.3 handshake. The single defense identified as effective was **aggressive stream multiplexing with many concurrent active streams**, which dropped detection by over 70 % — but only while multiplexing was active, not for a single lonely tunnel.

This single result reframes the field. **No deployed TLS-proxying tool fully solves it**; REALITY and Vision mitigate active probing and SNI analysis but still expose nested-handshake bursts. There is no confirmed report that the GFW has deployed Xue et al.'s classifier operationally as of April 2026, but community blocking reports since October 2022 — when hundreds of Trojan, Xray, VLESS, and V2Ray+TLS+WS servers on port 443 went dark within minutes to hours under sustained use — suggest at least a coarser variant is in production. Iran's MCI reportedly blocks REALITY servers after roughly two hours of heavy traffic (Xray-core discussions #2778, #3269), consistent with traffic-volume-based triggers layered atop TLS-in-TLS heuristics.

## The modern probe-resistant stack: VLESS + Vision + REALITY

**VLESS** is deliberately stateless. The client's first encrypted bytes are `version(1)=0 | UUID(16) | addons-length(1) | addons(ProtoBuf) | cmd(1) | port(2) | atyp(1) | addr(var) | payload`; there is **no protocol-layer encryption** because the outer transport (TLS, REALITY, gRPC+TLS, XHTTP) is assumed to supply confidentiality and integrity. Removing the inner cipher eliminates VMess's AEAD-over-AEAD fingerprint, and a 2025 optional **ML-KEM-768 + X25519 hybrid** wrapper adds post-quantum protection where transport trust is uncertain.

**XTLS-Vision (`xtls-rprx-vision`)** attacks the inner-handshake length distribution directly. The server runs a `TrafficState` machine that sniffs TLS record types to locate the client's inner ClientHello and Finished; before that boundary it injects random 0–900-byte **XtlsPadding** on records to flatten the distribution, and after Finished it **stops re-encrypting and hands the file descriptor to Linux `splice(2)`** for zero-copy kernel forwarding. The inner TLS 1.3 application-data records are then passed through verbatim rather than being re-framed, removing the nested-doll effect. Vision is consequential but partial: Xue et al. still detect it above 70 % in isolation.

**REALITY** is the cryptographic breakthrough. Instead of presenting its own certificate, a REALITY server impersonates a real site — typically something like `www.microsoft.com`. Configuration pins `privateKey` (X25519), accepted `serverNames`, eight-byte `shortIds`, and a `target`/`dest` pointing at the real site's `443`. The client sends a uTLS-forged ClientHello whose 32-byte `session_id` carries `timestamp(4) | shortId(8) | AES-GCM-encrypted(first-16B)` where the key is `HKDF-SHA256(ECDH(clientEphemeral, serverStaticPub), salt=Hello.Random[:20], info="REALITY")` with nonce = `Hello.Random[20:32]` and AAD = the raw ClientHello. The server runs the ECDH with its private key, decrypts, and on success **intercepts the handshake and forges an Ed25519-signed ephemeral certificate for the SNI**. On failure — or on any probe from an unauthorized source, including the GFW's probers and even a would-be MitM censor — the server **TCP-splices the connection verbatim to the real target** at microsoft.com, so the attacker receives that site's genuine certificate chain, correct OCSP, correct ALPN, correct timings.

This defeats three censorship layers simultaneously. It **breaks the SNI-whitelist** because the SNI is a real allowed name. It **breaks active probing** because an unauthorized probe receives a real-site response. And it **breaks MitM attempts** because the censor needs Microsoft's private key to present a matching cert. Known weaknesses: REALITY has **no defense against traffic-volume thresholds** (Iran MCI triggers after hours of sustained throughput), **IP/ASN-to-SNI mismatch is trivially anomalous** (a Hetzner IP serving `apple.com` is absurd), **REALITY is TCP-only** because QUIC's TLS 1.3 handshake has a zero-length session_id with no room for the authenticator, and the underlying TLS-in-TLS nested-handshake fingerprint of Xue et al. remains. Practitioners rotate `serverNames` against periodic SNI blocklists and pair REALITY with Vision to soften the nested-handshake shape.

## NaiveProxy: Chromium network stack as defense

**NaiveProxy** takes a radically different approach: rather than forge TLS, it *is* Chrome. The client is a ~0.3 % fork of Chromium's net stack linking real BoringSSL, real QUIC, real HTTP/2; the server is Caddy with a `forwardproxy@naive` module. Authentication rides on standard HTTP Basic via `Proxy-Authorization`; valid credentials reach the proxy backend, invalid ones are served by Caddy's regular `file_server` — so an unauthenticated probe hits a real static site. NaiveProxy's defining feature is its **padding layer**: the first `kFirstPaddings = 8` reads and writes are framed `uint16_BE orig_size | uint8 pad_size | data | zero-pad`, with `pad_size` uniform over 0–255; after eight operations padding turns off for throughput. CONNECT requests and responses include a `Padding:` header with 16–32 non-Huffman random bytes negotiating capability, and an END_STREAM DATA frame of length 48–72 is pre-inserted to conceal naive's anomalously high RST_STREAM rate.

The detection problem NaiveProxy solves cleanly is **TLS fingerprint parity**: because the library is literal Chrome, uTLS-equivalent fingerprint gaps (HTTP/2 SETTINGS frame order, record-layer buffering, QUIC Initial shape) do not exist — the fingerprint *is* Chrome's to the bit. Multiplexing many streams over HTTP/2 also places NaiveProxy closer to Xue et al.'s "works" regime on the nested-TLS attack. Drawbacks: Chrome's fingerprint rotates with every stable release roughly every six weeks, so running an outdated naive binary is itself a detection signal. **NaiveProxy was unaffected by the October 2022 TLS-circumvention block wave** and remains, alongside REALITY, one of the two most reliable primary transports for China in 2026.

## The fully-encrypted cohort: why Shadowsocks, VMess, and obfs4 fell

Pre-2017 Shadowsocks ran stream ciphers (`aes-256-cfb`, `chacha20`, `rc4-md5`) with an `EVP_BytesToKey` password KDF, no integrity, and ciphertext malleability that enabled the IMC 2020 active-probing attacks. The **AEAD redesign (SIP004, 2017)** chunked payload as `[len(2B) | len_tag(16B) | payload(≤0x3FFF B) | payload_tag(16B)]` under ChaCha20-Poly1305 or AES-GCM with a per-connection HKDF-SHA1 subkey, and **Shadowsocks-2022 (SIP022)** replaced HKDF-SHA1 with BLAKE3, added timestamped header chunks for ±30 s replay protection, bound response salts to request salts, and introduced Extensible Identity Headers for multi-user. What SIP022 did *not* fix was the on-the-wire appearance: fully random bytes with ~50/50 popcount, which is exactly what the 2021 GFW classifier flags. **Plain AEAD Shadowsocks and SIP022 are both reliably blocked on the GFW and on Iran's MCI/TCI**. The gfw-report popcount-padding fork — rolled into Shadowsocks-rust and Shadowsocks-android in October 2022 — manipulates the 0/1 bit ratio to stay inside the [3.4, 4.6] exemption band and remained effective as of early 2023.

**VMess** has a similar fate. Legacy VMess used MD5 HMAC auth and AES-128-CFB body with an MD5 IV over UTC-time epochs; a padding side-channel (issue v2fly#2523) enabled 16-probe deterministic detection. AEAD VMess (mandatory since 2022) uses `AES-128-ECB(EAuID)` and GCM-sealed headers with chunk-masking SHAKE-128. Plain TCP VMess still presents random bytes to the 2021 classifier and is blocked; **VMess+WS+TLS behind Cloudflare still partially works** but is vulnerable to TLS-in-TLS detection and Iran's AS13335 SNI blocking. **Trojan** likewise is not steganographic so much as authentic: a real TLS handshake, then 58 bytes of `hex(SHA224(password)) | CRLF` followed by a SOCKS5-style request; on any mismatch the server splices the socket to a preset fallback HTTP endpoint. Trojan's failure mode is TLS-in-TLS length analysis, and **TrojanProbe (Elsevier Computers & Security 2024)** showed subtle fingerprint differences between the Trojan fallback handler and real Nginx (header ordering, error codes). **Trojan-Go** extends it with smux multiplexing, WebSocket transport for CDN routing, an optional inner Shadowsocks-AEAD layer (important in China because domestic CDNs can MitM TLS), and uTLS; it still works via Cloudflare but is essentially unmaintained since 2022.

**obfs4** encodes a Curve25519 ntor handshake via **Elligator 2** to make its key representative indistinguishable from random, with HMAC-SHA256-128 keyed on (serverIdentity | NodeID | hour-epoch) against replay, NaCl secretbox framing with SipHash-2-4 OFB length masking, and optional IAT modes splitting packets for MTU fingerprint resistance. Its foundational assumption — that a censor cannot distinguish uniform-random ciphertext without the shared secret — **was invalidated by the 2021 classifier**. Default obfs4 bridges are effectively dead in both China and Iran; private bridges on randomized ports with IAT=1 sometimes still work. **ScrambleSuit** (Winter et al., WPES 2013), obfs4's predecessor, used UniformDH plus AES session tickets and polymorphic length/timing shaping; it was superseded by obfs4's faster ntor handshake and simpler design and is now only retained as a client shim.

## The QUIC cohort: Hysteria2 and TUIC

**Hysteria2** wraps QUIC (TLS 1.3, ALPN = `h3`) to masquerade as HTTP/3. Authentication is an H3 POST to `https://hysteria/auth` with `Hysteria-Auth: <password>`, returning rate-control and random padding headers; TCP streams become bidirectional QUIC streams, UDP becomes QUIC DATAGRAMs. The distinctive feature is **Brutal congestion control**: the client declares a target Mbps, and Brutal ignores RTT and loss signals, sending at fixed rate and compensating for measured loss by multiplying rate by 1/(1−loss) — literally brute-forcing throughput against throttling. **Salamander obfuscation** wraps each QUIC packet as `salt(8B) | XOR(QUIC_pkt, BLAKE2b-256(salt ‖ PSK))`, making the entire datagram opaque. **Port hopping** cycles source and destination UDP ports across a configured range every 10–30 s with iptables/nftables NAT on the server. Hysteria2's assumption — that UDP flows through and packet drops are throttling — fails in Iran 2025+ (UDP largely dropped on MCI/TCI) and is strained in China by the April 2024 QUIC-SNI extractor, so Salamander plus port-hopping is now required for it to work in China, and a TCP fallback (REALITY) is considered mandatory practice.

**TUIC v5** runs QUIC + TLS 1.3 with 0-RTT authentication: after the QUIC handshake, the client opens a unidirectional stream and sends `VER=5 | TYPE=0x00 | UUID(16) | TOKEN(32)`, where TOKEN is derived via the **TLS Keying Material Exporter** with label = UUID and context = raw password, binding the token to the current TLS session and preventing cross-session replay. Crucially the client does *not* wait for the auth response, dispatching Connect (TCP relay) and Packet (UDP relay with ASSOC_ID + PKT_ID + FRAG) commands in parallel; the server buffers and validates. TUIC has no built-in obfuscation layer, so it stands or falls on QUIC being allowed; in Iran post-June-2025 it fails with Hysteria2.

## uTLS and the JA3/JA4 game

**uTLS** (Frolov–Wustrow, NDSS 2019) forks Go's `crypto/tls` to expose a `ClientHelloSpec` permitting full control of cipher suites, extensions (order and content), supported groups, signature algorithms, ALPN, key_share, session_id pattern, GREASE, and — since Chrome's 2023 ClientHello-randomization change — extension permutation. It ships prebuilt specs for specific Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and iOS builds, each reproducing a specific JA3 (MD5 of version ‖ ciphers ‖ extensions ‖ curves ‖ EC-point-formats) and JA4 (FoxIO 2023 scheme: `t13d1516h2_...` encoding protocol/version/SNI-ALPN-cipher-count-extension-count/first-ALPN/sorted-ciphers/sorted-extensions/sig-algs). It does **not** model ChangeCipherSpec and Finished timing, HTTP/2 SETTINGS frame ordering (the Akamai fingerprint), or BoringSSL's record-layer buffering — precisely why NaiveProxy ships the full Chromium stack rather than uTLS alone. uTLS is the substrate under REALITY, Xray/VLESS, Trojan-Go, sing-box, and most Go-based tools and it defeats pure JA3/JA4 blocklists reliably, but it is necessary-not-sufficient against multi-signal fingerprinters.

## The Tor ecosystem: pluggable transports, Snowflake, and WebTunnel

The Pluggable Transports architecture is how Tor outsources camouflage. **PT 1.0** has Tor invoke the transport as a managed subprocess, passing configuration through environment variables (`TOR_PT_CLIENT_TRANSPORTS`, `TOR_PT_PROXY`, `TOR_PT_STATE_LOCATION`), reading newline-terminated messages (`VERSION`, `CMETHOD <transport> socks5 <addr:port>`, `CMETHODS DONE`) from the child, and routing Tor traffic to a localhost SOCKS5 listener with per-connection parameters in the SOCKS5 auth fields. On the bridge side, the PT uses Tor's Extended ORPort. **PT 2.0** generalized the spec away from Tor and moved per-connection params to JSON in SOCKS5 auth for larger blobs; **PT 3.0** supports in-process integration and UDP. Tor still uses PT 1.0; Lantern, Psiphon, and Operator Foundation's shapeshifter have adopted 2.0/3.0.

**Snowflake (Bocovich et al., USENIX Security 2024)** combines three components: a **broker** (matchmaker, reached by domain-fronted or AMP-cache rendezvous exchanging SDP offer/answer), ephemeral **proxies** running in browsers, extensions, or Orbot, and permanent **bridges**. Client-to-proxy transport is **WebRTC** (ICE with STUN for NAT traversal, DTLS + SCTP DataChannel). A Turbo Tunnel layer (KCP + smux) persists session state at the bridge, allowing clients to migrate between proxies mid-connection invisibly. The two exploited assumptions are a churning pool of ~100,000 volunteer proxies defeating IP enumeration, and that WebRTC is pervasive in Meet, Teams, and WhatsApp calls — so blocking it incurs unacceptable collateral damage. The **majority of Snowflake users are in Iran** after it became the principal Tor circumvention vector in 2022. **In China, default Snowflake bridges were blocked around 12 January 2024** via TLS fingerprinting of the broker rendezvous and DTLS flow characteristics (net4people/bbs #325); transient blocks had occurred in May 2023. Russia and Turkmenistan block periodically.

**WebTunnel**, announced by Tor on 12 March 2024, is the successor to meek. Based on Frolov's HTTPT design, it drops domain fronting entirely: the bridge operator runs a real HTTPS site with a valid public CA certificate, and the WebTunnel PT is reverse-proxied at a secret path (`/5m9yq0j4ghkz...`) via `Upgrade: websocket`, with every other path serving an ordinary website. Bridge lines include `webtunnel <ip>:<port> <FP> url=https://domain/SECRET`. WebTunnel exploits the *positive* shape test — deny-by-default whitelist regimes that the 2021 GFW classifier enforces *accept* real HTTPS — so it passes where obfs4 fails. By end-2024 roughly **143 bridges and a campaign targeting 200 by March 2025** were live, distributed through the HTTPS distributor only. **WebTunnel works in China and Russia but not in Iran**, where specific TLS-fingerprint filtering and SNI whitelist enforcement trip it up.

**Meek** and domain fronting are largely dead on major clouds. Google disabled it in April 2018, Amazon in April 2018, Cloudflare never allowed it, Microsoft Azure enforced a stricter block in January 2022 and tightened grandfathered configurations on 8 January 2024, and **Fastly blocked domain fronting on 27 February 2024**, ending `meek-azure`'s last CDN front. A 2023 survey found ~22/30 CDNs still permit some form of fronting (Akamai, CDN77, Lumen among them), and Snowflake's rendezvous has migrated toward AMP cache and Amazon SQS. Encrypted ClientHello does not fill the gap: ECH deployment is effectively Cloudflare-only (Niere et al., FOCI 2025), and **Russia has blocked all Cloudflare ECH-enabled services since November 2024**.

**Cloak** (cbeuw/Cloak) layers on top of Shadowsocks or OpenVPN. Its ClientHello carries a uTLS Chrome/Firefox/Safari fingerprint with the client's Curve25519 ephemeral pubkey hidden in the 32-byte Random field and authenticator in the Session ID, AES-GCM keyed by a shared secret. Unauthenticated connections are reverse-proxied transparently to a real `RedirAddr` site, defeating active probing. N (default 4) underlying TCP connections multiplex one logical session to mimic real browser parallelism. It remains popular for layering under Shadowsocks and OpenVPN in Iran. **Outline (Jigsaw)** is strictly Shadowsocks with mandated AEAD, plus probe-resistance patches (no position-dependent behavior at bytes 49–51, a 10k-entry replay cache, initial-packet size randomization via SOCKS-address merging, and a user-configurable prefix disguise such as a fake HTTP GET). Since RightsCon 2025, Outline's `outline-ss-server` also runs as a **Caddy module on `websocket-stream` / `websocket-packet` paths** with auto-TLS, routable through CDNs — the approach tested with ASL19's BeePass VPN in Iran. **Tor bridge distribution** switched from BridgeDB to **rdsys in October 2024**, with channels at `https` (captcha), `email` (Gmail/Riseup only), `telegram` (@GetBridgesBot `/bridges`), `settings` (autoconfig, which absorbed moat's bucket in September 2024), and the piloted anonymous-credential `lox` system. **Refraction Networking (TapDance, Conjure)** continues production deployment at Merit Network at ~140 Gbps serving over 1 million users via Psiphon, with **Conjure's phantom IPv6 decoys** avoiding TapDance's decoy-idle-timeout limits; it requires ISP cooperation that censors can route around (Schuchard, NDSS 2014).

## WireGuard and the rise of AmneziaWG

**Vanilla WireGuard has three DPI signatures that make it trivial to block**: every packet begins with a fixed 4-byte message-type field (`0x01,0x00,0x00,0x00` for init, `0x02` for response, `0x03` for cookie, `0x04` for data), the init packet is fixed at 148 bytes, the response at 92, and the cookie at 64. Egypt, Russia's TSPU (~12 % WG success rate), Iran's MCI/TCI (~98 % packet loss), and the GFW all detect it within seconds.

**AmneziaWG** leaves the Curve25519 / ChaCha20-Poly1305 / Noise IK core untouched and layers transport-level obfuscation through configurable parameters. **Jc/Jmin/Jmax** inject 0–N pseudorandom UDP packets of random size before each handshake (which happens roughly every 120 s). **S1 and S2** prepend random bytes to the init and response so that `len(init) = 148 + rand(0..S1)` and `len(resp) = 92 + rand(0..S2)` — with the constraint **S1+56 ≠ S2** because 148−92=56 and an equal padding difference would preserve the size relation. **AmneziaWG 2.0** adds **S3** (cookie junk, `len = 64 + rand(0..S3)`) and **S4** (data-packet junk applied to *every* data packet, the most impactful). **H1–H4** replace the fixed 32-bit message-type field; in 1.x they were fixed custom values, in 2.0 they are randomized per-packet over non-overlapping ranges (e.g., H1 ∈ 100000–800000, H2 ∈ 1000000–8000000). **I1–I5** define a signature chain of up to five custom protocol-signature UDP packets preceding each handshake, with tags `<b hex>`, `<c>`, `<t>` (timestamp), `<r length>` — I1 typically mimics a real QUIC Initial header, and **J1–J3** add further junk; **ITIME** is the signature-chain interval. Backward compatibility holds: if only Jc/Jmin/Jmax are set, junk packets precede a legitimate WG handshake that an unmodified server accepts. The kernel DKMS module achieves ~3 % overhead (95 → 92 Mbps); the userspace `amneziawg-go` is slower (the origin of earlier "65 % overhead" reports). AmneziaWG 1.5+ adds protocol masquerading as QUIC, DNS, SIP, or STUN.

**AmneziaVPN** packages this: users deploy their own VPS via Docker containers provisioned over SSH by the client, giving each installation a unique fingerprint (no universal signature) and supporting OpenVPN, WireGuard, IKEv2, OpenVPN-over-Cloak, OpenVPN-over-Shadowsocks, Shadowsocks standalone, AmneziaWG, and — in recent versions — Xray VLESS+REALITY. Related efforts include **wg-obfuscator** (simple userspace pad + STUN emulation) and the observation that, because WG handshakes are just the 148-byte init and 92-byte response every two minutes, routing only handshakes through a SOCKS5 tunnel suffices to bypass Egypt's block at near-zero overhead.

## Multi-protocol commercial and nonprofit systems

**Psiphon** is the most diversified system. Its core is SSH with pre-shared public-key server auth, wrapped in layered obfuscation, with clients making *multiple concurrent connection attempts across different protocol+server combinations and selecting the fastest*. The current `protocol/protocol.go` master list includes SSH, OSSH (obfuscated SSH), **TLS-OSSH** (with uTLS Chrome, Firefox, iOS, and Randomized-v2 fingerprints), SHADOWSOCKS-OSSH, UNFRONTED-MEEK-OSSH (and HTTPS / session-ticket variants), FRONTED-MEEK-OSSH over CDNs (including FRONTED-MEEK-QUIC-OSSH), QUIC-OSSH, TAPDANCE-OSSH, and CONJURE-OSSH. Anti-probing uses `TunnelProtocolPassthroughAddresses` to relay failed auth to a real target site; `OSSHObfuscatorSeedTransformSpecs` and `OSSHPrefixSplitMinDelay/MaxDelay` randomize initial bytes. **Lantern** runs a Flashlight proxy engine with historical domain fronting (largely retired), HTTPS fallback proxies on EC2, a **Kaleidoscope** trust-graph peer discovery system that originally used XMPP/Google Talk/OAuth 2.0 for bootstrap, and a newer **Kindling** library for small-data transfer. **AmneziaVPN** (above) sits in the same product category but differs structurally in being self-hosted.

## The strategic picture and what remains unsolved

Censorship in both countries has shifted from signature matching to **statistical shape analysis**. The GFW's 2021 fully-encrypted-traffic classifier ended the era of random-looking ciphertext; its 2024 QUIC-SNI extractor ended the era of assuming UDP was safe; Xue et al.'s 2024 TLS-in-TLS attack, even if not yet fully deployed at GFW scale, defines the next frontier. Iran's protocol whitelist plus 2025–2026 blackouts have collapsed the problem down to "look exactly like HTTPS to an allowed destination, or do not pass." Every tool that still works in April 2026 satisfies one of three properties: it **impersonates a real popular site with real cryptographic proofs** (REALITY), **is literally a browser stack** (NaiveProxy), or **rides on infrastructure the censor cannot break without crushing legitimate traffic** (Snowflake via WebRTC, WebTunnel via CA-signed HTTPS sites).

The unsolved problem is that **no deployed tool fully defeats Xue et al.'s encapsulated-handshake classifier**. Stream multiplexing with many active streams is the only known padding-free defense, and only while streams stay active. If the GFW deploys this classifier at full scale, REALITY+Vision, Trojan-Go, VLESS+TLS+WS, and most Iran-facing stacks would degrade simultaneously, forcing the field toward tools that never carry a nested handshake at all — natively-terminated HTTPS proxies like NaiveProxy, Conjure's phantom addresses, or WebTunnel-class operator-run sites. The Geedge Networks leak of September 2025 — 500+ GB of documents showing Chinese DPI exports to Myanmar, Pakistan, Ethiopia, and Kazakhstan — confirms this arms race is globalizing, with the TLS-in-TLS attack as the most likely next widely-exported capability. The circumvention community's response in 2026 is converging on **WATER** (FOCI 2024), a WebAssembly transport runtime that lets clients hot-swap transport logic independent of the host application, buying just enough agility to stay ahead of each new classifier by weeks rather than months.
