# Filtering the Flow: Technical Anatomy of Censorship and Circumvention in China and Iran

The 2023–2026 period marks an inflection in state-level Internet control. **China's Great Firewall (GFW) has transitioned from purely on-path injection to selective in-path UDP drops for QUIC** (April 2024), deployed a stateless "fully-encrypted traffic" classifier based on byte-level popcount and printable-ASCII heuristics (active since November 2021, escalated October 3, 2022), and spawned a regional provincial successor in Henan that eclipses the national blocklist. **Iran, meanwhile, has abandoned blacklisting in favor of national-gateway protocol whitelisting** — during the June 2025 twelve-day war and the 53-plus-day January 2026 blackout, only DNS/53, HTTP/80, and HTTPS/443 traversed the TIC chokepoint, while BGP announcements remained intact (a "stealth blackout" architecture). On the circumvention side, **REALITY + VLESS + XTLS Vision has become the consensus defense against the GFW**, Hysteria2 with Brutal congestion control dominates in lossy Iranian networks, and decoy routing (Conjure) has matured into a Psiphon-integrated fallback at a handful of U.S. university ISPs. The pattern is unmistakable: censors are moving from content identification to protocol/infrastructure denial; circumvention is moving from "look random" to "be indistinguishable from a real website."

What follows is a deep technical walk through each side of the arms race.

## Part 1 — The Great Firewall: architecture and mechanisms

### Topology, ASNs, and the on-path assumption

China's international connectivity is funneled through three state-controlled backbones: **AS4134 (CHINANET-BACKBONE / China Telecom)**, **AS4837 (CHINA169 / China Unicom)**, and **AS9808 (CMNET / China Mobile)**. Regional and sibling ASes implicated in injection or probing include AS4538 (CERNET), AS17622 (CNCGROUP Guangzhou), AS17816, AS9394, AS56046, AS56047, and AS7497 (CSTNET). Citizen Lab traceroute work (Marczak et al., 2015) localized GFW injection between hops ~17–18 from Berkeley toward Baidu, consistent with enforcement at ChinaNet/Unicom edge routers in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou.

The GFW is **overwhelmingly on-path (passive tap plus injection), not in-path (inline)**. SPAN/optical taps feed mirrored packets to censor devices that cannot drop the original; they can only emit additional forged packets. This has three consequences. First, legitimate packets still reach the far end, so the censor must *race* legitimate DNS replies or terminate TCP after the fact with RSTs. Second, because the censor observes both directions, it can synthesize in-window TCP sequence and ACK numbers, defeating RFC 5961's challenge-ACK. Third, everything UDP used to be handled purely by spoofed response injection — until **April 7, 2024**, when Zohaib et al. (USENIX Security 2025) documented the GFW's first known drop-based UDP blocking on QUIC. A second structural shift is **regional sub-national censorship**: Wang et al. (IEEE S&P 2025, *"A Wall Behind a Wall"*) identified a Henan provincial SNI/Host DPI box active since August 2023, which has **cumulatively blocked ~4.2 million domains — roughly five times the national GFW list** — and is stateless (bypassable by TCP segmentation and TLS record fragmentation).

### DNS injection: the workhorse, and Wallbleed

UDP/53 traffic crossing the border is inspected by **three concurrent injector processes** (Anonymous et al., FOCI 2020, "Triplet Censors") with distinct fingerprints. On blocklist match against the QNAME, each injector emits a forged A/AAAA response with the original QID, often an echoed IP-ID and TTL, and an answer section that may use a `c00c` compression pointer. Up to three forged responses race the legitimate answer; they normally win because GFW's RTT to Chinese clients is shorter than the recursive resolver's.

Hoang et al. (USENIX Security 2021) catalogued **more than 3,600 unique forged IPs** grouped into ~11 categories — some are deterministic sinkholes (4.36.66.178, 59.24.3.173, 8.7.198.45, 8.7.198.46, 203.98.7.65), others are real IPs belonging to Facebook (~44%), Twitter (~11%), and Dropbox (~10%) whose routing has been commandeered by the injection. AAAA responses are forged into the Teredo prefix `2001::/32`. Net4people issue #466 (Fifield, 2025) showed that **each injector cycles through its forged-IP pool in a deterministic order**, creating a side channel for counting active processes.

**Wallbleed** (Fan et al., NDSS 2025) is the most spectacular recent finding: a buffer over-read in the injector's DNS-name parser caused truncated 2-byte compression pointers to leak **up to 125 bytes of adjacent GFW process memory** in forged replies. Researchers recovered network headers, TLS handshakes in transit, x86_64 stack frames, and executable code — confirming the GFW runs on commodity x86_64 hardware. The vulnerability existed from at least October 2021, was partially patched November 2023 (Wallbleed v1), and fully patched March 2024 (Wallbleed v2). CERNET (AS4538) patched on its own schedule, evidence of semi-independent operation.

Encrypted DNS is handled protocol-by-protocol. **DoT (TCP/853)** is blocked outright by port and SNI. **DoH (HTTPS/443)** is blocked via SNI filtering of known resolver hostnames (`cloudflare-dns.com`, `dns.google`, `dns.nextdns.io`, `dns.quad9.net`); Niere et al. (PETS/FOCI 2025) measured **~36% of DoH queries from China censored** versus a ~6% global baseline. **DoQ (UDP/853)** became a target once QUIC SNI inspection went live in April 2024. **DNSCrypt** is blocked by server-IP blacklist and by its fixed magic bytes. **ODoH** is blocked transitively through SNI filtering of the proxy/target hostnames.

### IP blocking and pseudo-BGP blackholing

The GFW does **not** generally announce blackhole routes globally. Instead, target prefixes are dropped at the border via ingress ACLs or null-routed internally. Whole-prefix drops apply to Facebook (`31.13.0.0/16`, `157.240.0.0/16`), Twitter/X, Google (`142.250.0.0/15`, `172.217.0.0/16`, `216.58.192.0/19`), Instagram, and Telegram. For CDN-shared infrastructure (Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly), the GFW relies on SNI DPI rather than IP blackholing — wholesale IP blocks would collapse too much legitimate commerce. The predominant modern enforcement pattern is **dynamic, short-lived 3-tuple (srcIP, dstIP, dstPort) residual blocking** keyed to DPI triggers (see §6 below).

### TCP RST/RST+ACK injection

Documented since Clayton et al. (PETS 2006) and Weaver et al. (NDSS 2009). On DPI trigger, the censor emits forged packets spoofed as **both** endpoints: toward the client with source = server IP/port and a seq ≈ server's `SND.NXT`; toward the server with source = client and seq ≈ client's `SND.NXT`. **Three packets per direction** is typical, to defeat RFC 5961's ACK-challenge logic. Injector fingerprints include IP-ToS set to `0x68`, patterned IP-IDs (`16448`, mirrored, or incremented), and initial-TTL values that visibly differ from the legitimate server's — a classic injector tell. Importantly, **Wu et al. (USENIX Security 2023) showed the fully-encrypted-traffic blocker does not use RSTs**; it silently drops client→server packets on the 3-tuple for 120–180 seconds. RST injection persists for SNI-, Host-, and keyword-triggered blocking.

### TLS SNI, ESNI, ECH, and fingerprinting

The GFW parses TLS ClientHello on any destination port, reassembling TCP segments partially (Niere et al., CCS 2023 demonstrated that **TLS record-layer fragmentation across multiple ≤5-byte records reliably bypasses DPI** — GFW holds TCP-level state but not TLS record reassembly). On `server_name` blocklist match it injects RST/RST+ACK and enters residual censorship (120 or 180 s depending on vantage; timer **does not reset** on repeated triggers, unlike Iran's TSPU).

**ESNI (extension `0xffce`)**: blocking commenced July 29, 2020 (Bock et al., FOCI 2020). Drop all client→server packets for 120–180 s once seen; replacing the extension value with `0x7777` does not trigger.

**ECH (`0xfe0d` current, earlier `0xff02/03/04`)**: contrary to widespread folklore, **the GFW does not directly block the ECH extension in TLS ClientHello as of early 2026**. Niere et al. (PETS/FOCI 2025) tested ECH from 2024 into early 2025 and report no direct blocking; Zohaib et al. (USENIX Security 2025) confirm **GFW does not block QUIC payloads carrying ECH unless the outer decryptable SNI is forbidden**. The GFW kills ECH indirectly by blocking the DNS `HTTPS` RR lookup path (encrypted DNS to Cloudflare/NextDNS is blocked, so clients can't retrieve `ECHConfig`). Direct extension blocking is **Russia's TSPU behavior**, begun May 2024 after Cloudflare's September 2024 re-rollout.

**Fingerprinting**: Tor's custom TLS cipher set has triggered probes since 2011 (Winter & Lindskog, FOCI 2012). Modern circumvention tools therefore use **uTLS** to replay Chrome/Firefox/Safari ClientHellos bit-for-bit. JA3/JA3S is presumed in use; JA4/JA4+ at provincial boxes is plausible but not publicly confirmed. SNI-less TLS is *not* inherently blocked by the GFW.

### DPI patterns and fully-encrypted-traffic heuristics

Legacy protocol fingerprints in active use: **OpenVPN** opcodes `0x38`/`0x40` in the first payload byte (tls-auth-less connections blocked; tls-crypt connections anecdotally throttled to <56 kbit/s, though this figure is operator folklore, not peer-reviewed); **WireGuard** handshake-initiation `type=0x01` in byte 0 followed by three zero reserved bytes, total 148 B; **IPsec/IKE** on UDP 500/4500 with ISAKMP header; GRE/PPTP; L2TP on UDP 1701.

The central modern innovation is **Wu et al.'s fully-encrypted-traffic (FET) classifier** (USENIX Security 2023), deployed ~November 6, 2021 and escalated October 3, 2022 to a mass Shadowsocks block. It inspects the **first client→server data packet** after the 3-way handshake. Blocking is **probabilistic (~¼ of triggering connections)** to limit false positives and exploitability, and applies mainly on popular VPS hosting ranges (DigitalOcean, Vultr, Alibaba Cloud US, AWS Lightsail). The classifier applies five **exemption rules** — if any matches, the connection is considered non-encrypted and ignored:

- **Ex1 — popcount**: average set-bits-per-byte over the first packet. Exempt if the average lies outside [3.4, 4.6]. Real random/AEAD ciphertext clusters near 4.0; text and structured protocols skew away.
- **Ex2 — printable prefix**: exempt if the first 6 bytes are all in ASCII [0x20, 0x7e].
- **Ex3 — printable fraction**: exempt if more than 50% of bytes in the packet are printable.
- **Ex4 — printable run**: exempt if any contiguous run of ≥20 printable bytes exists.
- **Ex5 — protocol magic**: exempt on recognized TLS (`0x16 0x03 0x0{1,3}`), HTTP, SSH banner, SMTP etc.

A connection failing all five and whose source/destination falls in an affected range is blocked by 120/180-second client→server packet drops (server→client untouched; UDP unaffected until 2024). Circumvention tools responded within weeks (Shadowsocks-rust, Xray, V2Ray) by rewriting the first bytes to fake a TLS/HTTP magic (Ex5) or **popcount padding** to force the average into [3.4, 4.6] at ~17.6% worst-case overhead.

### Active probing

The GFW's active prober infrastructure has been documented since Winter & Lindskog (FOCI 2012) for Tor and comprehensively in Alice/Bob/Carol/Beznazwy/Houmansadr (IMC 2020) for Shadowsocks. Probes are sourced from **thousands of distinct Chinese IPs** (12,300 unique in Alice et al. over ~4 months; >22,000 across 2010–2015 in Ensafi et al.) across AS4837, AS4134, AS17622, AS17816, AS9808, AS56046/47. **TCP-timestamp (TSval) analysis** shows probes share a handful of linear sequences — suggesting a few dozen orchestrating processes behind the many source IPs.

Triggering is payload-length + entropy dependent: Shadowsocks-AEAD endpoints receive probes only when observed payloads exceed ~200 bytes. Alice et al. catalogued seven probe types, including exact-byte replays (R1–R5), random payloads of specific lengths (NR1: multiples of 8, 12, 16, 22, 33, 41, 49; NR2: exactly 221 B), and protocol-specific crafted handshakes (VMess, Trojan, obfs4, SoftEther). Probes arrive ~once every 10 minutes per port and can recur as late as 3 weeks after the original capture.

Countermeasures: **probe-resistant proxies** — Frolov et al. (NDSS 2020) formalized the requirement that a server remain **bit-silent** until the client proves knowledge of a shared secret. This is the core design principle in Shadowsocks-2022, Trojan-TLS (fallback to real site), NaïveProxy, and VLESS+REALITY.

### QUIC / HTTP3 in 2024–2026

Until April 2024, the GFW ignored QUIC content and blocked only by IP (collateral with Google). **On April 7, 2024**, Zohaib, Sippe, Houmansadr & Wustrow (USENIX Security 2025, Honorable Mention) documented the GFW beginning to inspect QUIC Initial packets, derive initial keys via the RFC 9001 HKDF with the published salt, decrypt the TLS ClientHello inside the CRYPTO frame, extract SNI, and match against a **QUIC-specific blocklist** approximately 55–60% the size of the DNS blocklist (11,854 of 58,207 QUIC-blocked Tranco domains are blocked *only* over QUIC).

Mechanics: single-packet trigger → the GFW **drops all subsequent client→server UDP** on the 3-tuple for **180 seconds** (first known UDP drop-based blocking, confirming some in-path capability). Blocking is probabilistic and load-sensitive; latency from trigger to block is 60 ms to 7.5 s with >90% within 1 second. Censorship intensifies between 03:00–06:00 Beijing time. The GFW uses UDP-4-tuple flow tracking with a 60 s timeout, and **only inspects flows where UDP sport > dport** (captures ~90% of QUIC clients while halving state). Bypasses documented include: **QUIC v2 (different initial salt) passes unblocked**; Chrome's Chaos Protection scrambles and fragments CRYPTO frames across UDP datagrams, defeating the single-packet parser; ML-KEM/Kyber ClientHellos exceed MTU and fragment the same way. Disclosure to Chinese authorities in January 2025 prompted behavior changes in March 2025.

### Flow and timing analysis

Residual censorship is the GFW's universal timing primitive: 120 or 180 s on the 3-tuple, with capacity-pressure effects shortening individual durations when the state table fills. Long-lived blocking (30+ minutes, sometimes indefinite) is applied to newly detected circumvention servers and around politically sensitive dates (October 1, June 4, March 5, party congresses). Bock et al. (WOOT 2021) demonstrated that **residual censorship is weaponizable as a denial-of-service primitive**: a forged spoofed trigger packet suffices to block a target's access for 3 minutes.

Beyond this stateless blocker, newer detection research includes **Xue et al. (USENIX Security 2024)** on encapsulated-TLS-handshake fingerprinting (detects tunnels that carry an inner TLS handshake through the obfuscation with **>70% TPR at 0.054% FPR**); **OpenVPN fingerprinting** via opcode/ACK-pattern histograms over the first 10 packets (Xue et al., USENIX Security 2022); and **cross-layer RTT** fingerprinting (NDSS 2025) that correlates transport-layer RTT with application-layer RTT to reveal proxy relays.

### The Great Cannon

The Great Cannon (Citizen Lab, 2015) is a **distinct, in-path** system co-located with GFW hops. Its 2015 attacks on GreatFire.org CloudFront instances and GitHub (`greatfire` and `cn-nytimes` repos) worked by MITM-substituting the responses to Baidu's analytics and ad-tracking JavaScript (`hm.baidu.com`, `pos.baidu.com`, `cbjs.baidu.com`) for users outside China. GC replaced ~1.75–2% of requests (spiking to 17.5% during the attack) with malicious JS that issued repeated requests to the targets, turning foreign visitors of any Baidu-integrated site into unwitting DDoS bots — 2.6 billion requests/hour at peak; GreatFire's AWS bill hit ~$30,000/day. Post-2015 HTTPS adoption has largely defanged GC's content-substitution vector; **no major public GC events have been documented since**, though the infrastructure could deliver browser exploits rather than DDoS scripts if reactivated.

### 2024–2026 developments, summarized

The post-2023 trend is **towards stateless cheap heuristics (popcount, printable bytes, exact-match SNI) plus UDP-drop capability for QUIC**, not heavy ML classifiers. Infrastructure leaks are reshaping the threat model: on **September 11, 2025**, >500 GB of source code and internal communications leaked from **Geedge Networks and the MESA Lab at IIE/CAS** (Fang Binxing's circle — GFW R&D core), with GFW Report analysis revealing export of Chinese censorship platforms to Myanmar, Pakistan, Ethiopia, and Kazakhstan under Belt and Road. On **August 20, 2025**, the GFW briefly injected unconditional RST+ACK packets on all TCP/443 traffic for ~74 minutes (GFW Report analysis), a rare failure mode but a reminder of the GFW's ability to deny port 443 at will.

## Part 2 — Iran: national gateway as censorship substrate

### SHOMA / National Information Network and the TIC chokepoint

Iran's censorship architecture is built atop the **National Information Network (NIN / *Shabake-ye Melli-ye Ettela'āt*, SHOMA)**, launched 2013 and formally unveiled August 28, 2016. The governance stack descends from the **Supreme Council of Cyberspace (SCC)** through the **National Cyberspace Center**, **Ministry of ICT (MCI)**, and its operational arms: the **Communications Regulatory Authority (CRA)**, the wholesale **Telecommunication Infrastructure Company (TIC)** — which holds a **monopoly on international gateways and operates AS12880, AS49666, AS48159, and AS58224** — and the retail **Telecommunication Company of Iran (TCI, now IRGC-linked via Etemad Mobin consortium)**. Downstream, AS44244 (MTN Irancell), AS197207 (MCI/Hamrah-e Aval), AS57218 (RighTel), AS31549 (Aria Shatel), AS16322 (Pars Online), AS39308/AS43754 (AsiaTech), and AS202468 (ArvanCloud) operate under TIC transit. The sole exception is **AS6736 (IPM academic gateway)**, which retains independent international transit for research traffic.

Below TIC, the architecture interposes **"Secure Border Gateways"** (*گذرگاه‌های امن مرزی*) at the NIN/international boundary, and **"Internal Secure Gateways"** within access and core layers for intra-NIN filtering that works even when international links are severed. Enforcement is centralized: Tai et al.'s **IRBlock** measurement (USENIX Security 2025; 700 M domains, 11 M IPs probed from Dec 2024 to Jan 2025) and Aryapour's TTL tracing (arXiv:2507.14183, 2025) show **all interference packets originate from the same hop** — a stark contrast with the GFW's three-middlebox architecture.

### Protocol whitelisting, not blacklisting

Iran's pre-2019 posture was SmartFilter (McAfee/Secure Computing)–style URL/keyword blacklisting plus DNS poisoning. Bock et al. (FOCI 2020) first identified Iran's crisis-mode **protocol whitelister** that permits only DNS/UDP 53, HTTP/TCP 80, and HTTPS/TCP 443 across the national edge. This has solidified over 2023–2026. During the **June 2025 Israel–Iran war "stealth blackout,"** TTL-limited tracing showed all other protocols — OpenVPN/1194, IKE/500, generic UDP/TCP on non-standard ports, SSH/22 to foreign hosts, MQTT/1883 — silently dropped without RST or ICMP. QUIC/UDP 443 was blocked (Cloudflare Radar: HTTP/3 share on AS58224 collapsed from ~40% to <5% by January 3, 2026). In the **January 2026 post-protest phase**, whitelisting became the default operational posture: the global Internet is blocked and only specifically authorized services are reachable. Filterwatch calls this "Absolute Digital Isolation" or "Barracks Internet"; ordinary Iranians now seek a *melli-shekan* (national-network-breaker), not merely a *filter-shekan* (VPN).

The operational implication is severe for circumvention: **fingerprinting becomes irrelevant when default-deny is the policy.** No matter how perfectly a proxy mimics Chrome — if its egress port or destination IP falls outside the allowed tuple set, the packets vanish.

### Throttling and tiered bandwidth

Iran implements bandwidth shaping at TIC gateways and at carrier aggregation. International bandwidth is a scarce, taxed resource: after the Instagram/WhatsApp blocks in September 2022, TIC raised international tariffs 34% in FY 2023–24 to offset revenue loss (Filterwatch). Protocol-selective shaping degrades HTTPS handshake completion below 50% during protest-period mobile curfews (OONI/M-Lab). Domestic destinations on ArvanCloud (AS202468) and Iranian data centers get half-price or unmetered quotas — an explicit economic pull toward the NIN. DDoS-induced disruption in late March–April 2024 produced >80% network degradation and ~40% packet loss; analysis identified TIC as the chokepoint source, prompting a recommendation to raise DDoS-mitigation capacity from 200 Gbps to 1,800 Gbps.

### SNI filtering, HTTP injection, and the DigiNotar legacy

Iran's TLS SNI blocking injects **RST+ACK toward the client** after observing a blocklisted ClientHello — single-middlebox enforcement, in contrast to GFW's triplicated injectors. Distinctive local features include **case-sensitive keyword matching** on HTTP Host and URLs (Lange et al. 2025 — evadable by case-flipping or fragmentation) and **dual-layer checks** where OONI has observed Iran blocking `www.instagram.com` via both SNI inspection and unencrypted Server Certificate SAN/CN inspection. Three distinct DNS poisoning signatures (10.10.34.34, 10.10.34.35, 10.10.34.36) indicate three separate DNS injectors; one exhibits GFW-like TTL reflection.

HTTP block-page injection remains canonical: blocked HTTP/80 requests receive a `403` with an iframe to `http://10.10.34.34`. The historical apex of HTTPS interception was **DigiNotar / "Operation Black Tulip"** in July–August 2011: a Dutch CA compromise produced a fraudulent `*.google.com` wildcard and 530+ other certificates; ~300,000 Iranian Gmail users were intercepted over about a month before Chrome's hardcoded Google pin (the `alibo` user's first report, August 28, 2011) broke the case. DigiNotar collapsed into bankruptcy in September 2011. Post-2011, CT logs, HSTS, HPKP/pin evolution, and CAA records raised the cost; Iran has pivoted to **pressuring users onto "governable" domestic apps** (Soroush, Rubika, Bale, Eitaa, Shad, "Telegram Talaei") with server-side state access and to forks that ship extra root CAs.

### Shutdowns executed at BGP and data-plane layers

**November 2019 "Bloody Aban":** ordered by the Supreme National Security Council after fuel-price protests. Shutdown began 14:00 UTC on November 16, 2019; IODA recorded a ~33% drop in globally routed /24s initially. Mobile carriers withdrew BGP first, fixed-line ISPs followed over ~5 hours; crucially, **AS12880 (ITC) did not withdraw BGP — but its data plane went to zero**, indicating null-routing and ACLs at the TIC gateway rather than route withdrawal. Near-total blackout ~5.5–7 days; economic cost $1.0–1.5 billion (ARTICLE 19).

**September 2022 (Mahsa Amini):** architecturally different. Instead of BGP withdrawals, Iran executed **nightly mobile-only curfews** on AS44244, AS57218, and AS197207, ~16:00 to midnight IRST for 13 consecutive nights, cumulating ~100 hours of mobile blackout. Simultaneously Instagram/WhatsApp were fully blocked (Sept 21), DoH/DoT blocked (Sept 20), QUIC/HTTP3 blocked (Sept 22), and region-level blackouts hit Sanandaj and parts of Tehran.

**June 2025 twelve-day war "stealth blackout":** **No BGP withdrawals.** Routes remained announced globally while at the TIC chokepoint: DNS poisoning, case-sensitive HTTP injection, SNI-based RST, and country-wide protocol whitelist dropped traffic ~90%. Psiphon retained ~1.5 M users (≈⅓ baseline); Lantern's proxyless transport retained ~40% of traffic; VLESS+REALITY, AmneziaWG, and Shadowsocks-2022 with plugins were the most resilient.

**January 2026 near-total blackout:** began 18:45 UTC on January 8, 2026. Even NIN was briefly cut. 53 consecutive days of near-total blackout (by April 21, 2026) — the longest recorded nationwide Internet disruption anywhere. Partial restoration and a "whitelist new normal" began January 26, 2026; by end-March, connectivity hovered at ~1% of baseline. Minister of ICT Hashemi reported **$35.7 M/day direct cost**; total $70–80 M/day with indirect. Approximately 50,000 Starlink terminals went active; ~6,000 more were smuggled in post-January; Iran's police claimed 40,000 Starlink links severed by January 20 using Russian **Krasukha and Tirada-2S** RF jamming systems (300 km dead zones) and GPS spoofing (30–80% packet loss).

### VPN protocol blocking

Iran's DPI targets well-known VPN fingerprints. **WireGuard** handshake-initiation packets (`type=0x01`, 148 B total, fixed reserved-zero pattern) are throttled within minutes and then blocked on default port UDP 51820; rekeying to 443/UDP helps briefly. Obfuscated variants — **AmneziaWG, swgp-go, NordLynx-wrapped** — remain usable on some paths. **OpenVPN** is near-100% detected via the `P_CONTROL_HARD_RESET_CLIENT_V2` opcode byte and TLS-auth HMAC pattern; even TCP/443 mode is blocked without an obfuscation wrapper (`obfsproxy`, Stunnel). **IKEv2/IPsec** UDP/500 and 4500 fail under the whitelist regardless of ISAKMP masking. **L2TP** on UDP/1701 and **PPTP** (GRE) are dropped by default. **SSH/22** to foreign IPs received a blanket block ahead of the July 2024 elections (Filterwatch, August 2024). Active probing does occur but less aggressively than GFW's; Iran leans on statistical traffic analysis and TLS JA3/JA4 fingerprinting of circumvention clients.

### CDN blocking: the Cloudflare problem

Irancell (AS44244) blocked **all Cloudflare IPs on April 16, 2023** (net4people #245); MCI and TCI followed. Selective blocking within Cloudflare ranges (e.g., 188.114.96.3 blocked, 188.114.97.3 not) has persisted for years. Cloudflare Workers (`*.workers.dev`) began returning 10.10.34.36 by mid-2023. **SNI-conditional blocking** on Cloudflare IPs was observed in October 2022: non-Cloudflare SNIs to a Cloudflare IP failed, but `www.cloudflare.com` succeeded (net4people #133). In 2024 Cloudflare's own firewall upgrade began flagging V2Ray/VLESS patterns as HTTP DDoS, which disrupted Iranian user-provisioned tunnels (net4people #429). **Fastly** was open through much of 2024 for VLESS-over-WebSocket but was increasingly shaped and blocked through 2025. Domestic **ArvanCloud** (sanctioned by U.S. Treasury in August 2024 for supporting state censorship) is the de facto whitelisted CDN — its IP ranges enjoy "Iran Access" pricing.

### Tiered internet: White SIMs and the National Portal

Iran has formalized a **class-based access model**: standard residential SIMs face full filtering and protocol whitelisting; a **business tier** ("International Stable Internet") grants stable international connectivity to registered companies and exporters via IP registration at the Chamber of Commerce; academic access routes via IPM AS6736; **"White SIMs"** (*سیم‌کارت سفید*) issued to hardliners, IRGC, state media, judges, and select journalists provide unfiltered access without VPN; a dedicated **government tier (Special ICT Zone)** peers directly with the international gateway behind Secure Border Gateways and was linked to the late-2025 bulk scraping of Instagram follower lists identified by Meta/Cloudflare. The November 2025 X "About This Account" feature exposed numerous Iranian officials using White SIMs while publicly supporting censorship; several were disabled in December 2025 after the embarrassment. The **National Portal for Iranians** (launched February 19, 2024) registers every Iranian from birth and binds identity to SIM, IP, and traffic class for DPI enforcement — a pre-condition for tier allocation.

### Recent legislation and 2024–2026 developments

The **User Protection Bill (*Tarh-e Sianat*)**, originally 2018, was re-submitted November 19, 2025; principal provisions have been implemented piecemeal via SCC statutes and Seventh Development Plan articles. The February 2024 SCC *Statute for Countering Filter Breakers* (Art. 6) prohibits unlicensed VPN use and creates a licensing body for "sanctioned-breaking tools" — state-approved VPNs only. A July 2025 *Bylaw to Facilitate the Economy of Digital Businesses* formalizes the tiered access model; a July 2025 *Bill on Combating False News Content in Cyberspace* was submitted double-urgency; the late-2025 **Anti-Espionage Law** criminalizes Starlink possession (6 months–2 years standard; **death penalty** for use with espionage or *mofsed-fel-arz* framings). DPI vendors are in flux: **Sandvine PacketLogic** was U.S.-sanctioned February 2024 after Iran/Belarus documentation; the replacement is **Russian Protei** (Citizen Lab 2023 documented Protei at Ariantel; Global Voices 2026 confirmed continuing deployment), with evidence from the Geedge/MESA leak that Chinese-origin platforms are also being pushed into Iran's orbit. **IPv6 blocking** began in May 2024.

## Part 3 — The circumvention stack in 2024–2026

### V2Ray / Xray-core: VMess, VLESS, and XTLS Vision

**VMess (legacy)** uses a stateless UUID-derived authentication. The client header embeds a 16-byte `EAuID` = AES-128-ECB encryption of `timestamp(8) ‖ random(4) ‖ CRC32(12)` under an HKDF-like key derived from the UUID; an 18-byte `ELength` field protects the variable header length under AES-128-GCM with AAD=EAuID; the variable header is GCM-sealed and contains version, request IV, per-session symmetric key, response auth, options, padding, security byte, SOCKS-like address command, and an FNV-1a-32 checksum. A ±120 s anti-replay window plus random-duration "draining" on auth failure add probe resistance. VMess's body uses AES-128-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305. **In practice VMess is deprecated** — its small, high-entropy header is detected by the GFW's FET classifier, and naive WebSocket/TCP transports expose it to active probing.

**VLESS** is stripped down by design: no transport-layer crypto, no anti-replay, header `version(1) ‖ UUID(16) ‖ addon_len(1) ‖ addon ‖ cmd(1) ‖ port(2) ‖ addr_type(1) ‖ addr ‖ payload`, with security declared `"none"`. All authenticity is delegated to the outer stream — usually TLS 1.3 + REALITY + uTLS. The `flow` field selects post-handshake behavior: `xtls-rprx-vision` is the current value. Recent Xray adds an optional **ML-KEM-768 + X25519 hybrid PQ KEM** wrapper and an **ML-DSA-65** signature option for REALITY's ephemeral certificate.

**XTLS Vision** targets the **TLS-in-TLS fingerprint**. When a browser's HTTPS session is tunneled through an outer TLS connection to the proxy, an observer sees a stereotyped sequence: ClientHello-sized record (~517 B), ServerHello+Cert (multi-KB), Finished, ApplicationData — twice. Vision operates as a wrapping state machine: it detects the inner ClientHello (content type `0x16`, version `0x0303`), pads the first few outer records (900–1,400 B random), inserts a UUID signal packet after handshake, and then executes `splice(2)` (Linux zero-copy) on the outer TCP socket — the outer TLS records continue but proxy-side encrypt-decrypt stops. The outer shape now mirrors the inner stream. Vision is only supported over raw TCP or REALITY (not H2/gRPC/WS, which have their own framing) and must be paired with uTLS.

### REALITY — the current GFW defense of choice

REALITY eliminates three weaknesses at once: the need to own a domain and manage a certificate, the fingerprint of custom Go/OpenSSL TLS stacks, and the unknown-SNI/cert-mismatch probe signal. It is a patched `crypto/tls` fork on the server, uTLS on the client.

**How it works.** The client sends a TLS 1.3 ClientHello whose `serverName` is in the server's allowlist (e.g., `www.microsoft.com`). Its `key_share` X25519 public is computed normally, but the private scalar is derived from `HKDF(shared_secret, "REALITY")` where `shared_secret = X25519(client_ephem, server_static_pub)`. The authentication token is stuffed into the **SessionID** (32 bytes, ignored by ordinary servers): `SessionID[0:8] = timestamp`, `[8:16] = shortID`, `[16:32] = AES-GCM tag` over the ClientHello outer bytes keyed by the derived auth key. The server extracts SessionID, recomputes the ECDHE with its X25519 private key, verifies the tag. **On success**, the server terminates TLS locally, generating an *ephemeral trusted certificate* the authenticated client can deterministically reconstruct and verify; the VLESS+Vision session proceeds. **On failure** — a probe, an unlucky browser, any unauthenticated traffic — the server acts as a **transparent TCP relay to the configured `dest`** (e.g., `www.microsoft.com:443`): it forwards the original, unmodified ClientHello bytes to the real server and proxies the real ServerHello, Certificate (with real CA chain, real SCTs), and Finished back. **The prober sees a real, valid, CT-logged certificate chain terminating at the real site.** Because that site isn't blockable, the REALITY server isn't distinguishable from it to the censor.

Configuration essentials: `dest` should be same-ASN, serve TLS 1.3 + HTTP/2, not redirect, ideally present encrypted handshake after ServerHello (dl.google.com-class) with OCSP stapling. `privateKey`/`publicKey` are X25519; `shortIds` are 0–8-byte identifiers for per-client granularity; `maxTimeDiff` bounds replay; the client uses `fingerprint: "chrome"` (uTLS) and `spiderX` fallback path.

**Known limitations.** There is no cryptographic break of REALITY; the shared-secret handshake is sound. But:
- **Xue et al. (USENIX Security 2024)** encapsulated-TLS-handshake fingerprinting achieves >70% TPR / 0.054% FPR against REALITY+VLESS+Vision on Merit Network — authenticated clients still tunnel inner TLS handshakes, and packet size/direction bursts survive padding. Stream multiplexing (Mux.Cool) reduces detection by >70% at the cost of HOL blocking.
- **Cross-layer RTT** fingerprinting (NDSS 2025) correlates transport and application RTT to reveal relays.
- **Dest mismatches** (redirect, TLS 1.2 only, different ASN than server) produce observable anomalies.
- **Operator mistakes**: X25519 public-key reuse across many server IPs (visible in public proxy-list repos) enables IP clustering; fallback rate limiting differs from the real dest's behavior; the stolen cert chain must exceed ~3,500 bytes to match Chrome's post-ML-KEM ClientHello size distribution.
- `RealiTLScanner` (from the XTLS team itself) demonstrates the feasibility of scanning for REALITY-like servers via active probing.

REALITY is best framed as "raising the cost of blocking," not as undetectability. **It must not be combined with non-XTLS proxy protocols** (Shadowsocks-over-REALITY, Trojan-over-REALITY) — these reintroduce the inner-TLS-in-outer-TLS pattern Vision was designed to erase.

### Shadowsocks family

**Original SS** (RC4-MD5/AES-CFB) is broken and deprecated — active chosen-ciphertext probing by the GFW fingerprinted it through 2015–2019. **SS-AEAD (SIP004)** — `aes-128-gcm`, `aes-256-gcm`, `chacha20-ietf-poly1305`, `xchacha20-ietf-poly1305` — uses an `EVP_BytesToKey(password)` master key (MD5-based, weak), HKDF-SHA1-derived per-connection subkeys from a fresh salt, and AEAD chunks `[len(2)][len_tag(16)] || [payload(≤0x3FFF)][payload_tag(16)]`. Still detected by Wu et al.'s FET classifier and by active replay.

**Shadowsocks-2022 (SIP022/SIP023)** is a full redesign:
- Ciphers `2022-blake3-aes-{128,256}-gcm` and `2022-blake3-chacha20-poly1305`; PSKs are fixed-length CSPRNG-generated and base64-encoded (no password stretching).
- KDF is `BLAKE3::derive_key("shadowsocks 2022 session subkey", PSK ‖ salt)`.
- TCP request header carries a `type(1)=0x00 ‖ timestamp(8) ‖ length_of_variable_header(2)` fixed prefix, then a variable header with `padding_len ‖ socks_addr ‖ padding ‖ initial_payload`. The response header echoes a `request_salt(32)` to bind response to request — preventing replay and splicing. ±30 s timestamp window plus Bloom-filter replay cache.
- UDP (AES variants) uses 16-byte "separate header" (session ID + packet ID) AES-128-ECB-encrypted with PSK, then AES-GCM body with BLAKE3-derived subkey; sliding-window replay filter per session.
- **EIH (SIP023)** enables multi-user with identity-onion: each relay peels `AES(iPSK_n, BLAKE3(iPSK_{n+1})[:16])` toward the final user PSK.

SS-2022 outer traffic is statistically indistinguishable from random; deployed inside a WebSocket+TLS transport or behind Cloak or ShadowTLS, it remains a primary workhorse. **Outline (Jigsaw/Google)** wraps SS-AEAD (and SS-2022 since 2024) in a consumer-oriented deployment but adds no protocol innovation.

### Trojan, Trojan-Go

Trojan *depends on a real, valid TLS certificate* (Let's Encrypt typical). The server is an ordinary TLS 1.2/1.3 endpoint on 443. After the outer handshake, the first client record begins with `hex(SHA224(password)) ‖ CRLF ‖ CMD(1) ATYP(1) DST.ADDR DST.PORT ‖ CRLF ‖ payload`. Any structural mismatch or unknown hash causes the server to transparently proxy the TCP connection to a `fallback_addr:port` (default local nginx serving an innocuous site). A scanner probing the endpoint sees a real website served by the cover nginx. The weakness is plaintext authentication inside the outer TLS: all security hinges on PFS-enabled TLS 1.3. **Trojan-Go** added Smux multiplexing, WebSocket for CDN fronting, and optional inner Shadowsocks — all now superseded by Vision/REALITY.

**TrojanProbe** (Feng et al., *Computers & Security* vol. 148, 2024) distinguishes trojan, trojan-go, and trojan-r implementations by crafted HTTP-probe responses revealing implementation-specific behavior.

### Hysteria / Hysteria2 (QUIC + Brutal)

Hysteria is custom QUIC on `quic-go`, UDP-only, typically port 443, ALPN `h3`. **Hysteria2** switches auth to an **HTTP/3 masquerade**: a POST to `https://<server>/auth` with `Hysteria-Auth`, `Hysteria-CC-RX`, `Hysteria-Padding` headers; success returns a deliberately non-standard status `233` with matching headers. Failed auth must either serve a normal webserver or reverse-proxy upstream via the `masquerade` config. Relay traffic uses QUIC streams (`[0x401(varint) ‖ addr_len ‖ addr ‖ pad_len ‖ pad]`) for TCP; QUIC datagrams with a session ID for UDP.

**Brutal congestion control** is the defining feature. Instead of probing for capacity (BBR) or reacting to loss (CUBIC/Reno), Brutal **takes the user-declared rate as target, paces packets at that rate regardless of loss, measures loss periodically, and compensates by sending at `rate / (1 − p)`**. This is devastatingly effective on **lossy, shared, or deliberately-degraded links** (Iran): when a censor drops a percentage of UDP packets to throttle VPNs, BBR-based protocols collapse; Brutal just sends more, achieving higher goodput at the cost of being unfair to TCP neighbors. A companion kernel module **TCP-Brutal** ports it to TCP via `TCP_CONGESTION` sockopt. Optional **Salamander** obfuscation XORs UDP datagrams with `BLAKE2b(key ‖ nonce)`, producing random-looking bytes. **Port hopping** rotates UDP source/dest ports (iptables DNAT on the server) to defeat port-specific UDP blocking.

Hysteria2 still works broadly in China as of 2025 but net4people #528 flags it as detectable via cross-layer RTT when using BBR without Brutal. In Iran its effectiveness depends on UDP reaching the server at all — during protocol-whitelist phases, any UDP egress beyond DNS is dropped.

### TUIC v5, NaïveProxy, Cloak, mieru, gost, brook

**TUIC v5** rides inside QUIC with ALPN `h3`. Commands are `VER(1=0x05) ‖ TYPE(1) ‖ OPT`. Authentication opens a uni-stream with `UUID(16) ‖ TOKEN(32)`; `TOKEN` is derived via TLS keying-material export labeled by UUID, context = raw password — binding auth to the TLS 1.3 master secret. TCP relay uses `Connect` (type 0x01) over a bi-directional stream; UDP relay uses `Packet` (type 0x02) in two modes: **native** (QUIC datagrams — lossy, no HOL) or **quic** (QUIC streams — reliable, ordered). QUIC 0-RTT is supported. Congestion control is client-selectable among CUBIC/Reno/BBR.

**NaïveProxy** uses Chromium's `//net` stack — its TLS ClientHello is identical to the exact Chrome release: same extensions, GREASE placement, ALPS, ECH, key_share groups. **Passes JA3/JA4/JA4H because it is Chrome.** Transport is HTTP/2 (or HTTP/3) `CONNECT` to a patched Caddy `forwardproxy@naive` fork; unauthenticated requests are routed to static content or an upstream probe-resistance target. NaïveProxy adds a small **padding protocol** on the first 8 reads/writes per H2 stream (`orig_hi ‖ orig_lo ‖ pad_len ‖ orig ‖ zeros`) to defeat early-byte size analysis. **HTTP Fast CONNECT** sends payload immediately after CONNECT without waiting for 200 response. Residual TLS-in-TLS fingerprint remains, which NaïveProxy explicitly documents as unsolvable without MITM.

**Cloak** is a Shadowsocks/OpenVPN/Tor plugin that disguises traffic as TLS 1.2. Its ClientHello places an X25519-AES-256-GCM sealed `user_id ‖ timestamp ‖ random` token in the 32-byte SessionID; extension order mimics Chrome/Firefox via `BrowserSig`. On auth failure the server proxies to `RedirAddr` — a real cover site. Multiplexes `NumConn` (default 4) sessions to spread metadata. **mieru** (enfein) takes a different path: full traffic randomization, XChaCha20-Poly1305 keys derived per-minute via `SHA256(password ‖ 0x00 ‖ username ‖ unixTime_min)` with ±1-min tolerance; segments carry random-non-encrypted padding that adjusts length distribution and printable-ratio entropy; **decryption-failure behavior is non-deterministic** (response timing, byte count, and padding vary by server-seed), frustrating ML probe classifiers. **brook** uses ChaCha20-Poly1305 with per-connection nonce and BLAKE2b KDF; multiple transport modes. **gost** is a Swiss-army proxy chain supporting everything (SOCKS5/SS/Trojan/KCP/QUIC/obfs4) as chainable inbounds/outbounds.

### Meek / domain fronting — effectively dead

Domain fronting (SNI=allowed front, Host=blocked backend) was shut down across major CDNs: **Cloudflare** (never officially supported; broke ~2015; fully blocked SNI/Host mismatch 2020), **Google/App Engine** (April 14, 2018), **Amazon CloudFront** (April 27, 2018), **Microsoft Azure** (announced 2021, enforced late 2022, grandfather deadline January 8, 2024), **Fastly** (announced October 2023, enforced February 27, 2024). A 2023 survey found smaller CDNs (Akamai, CDN77, Lumen) still permitting some form of fronting for 22 of 30 tested. Workarounds: **Cloudflare Workers + WebSocket** (not SNI-mismatch fronting but IP-sharing via the CF edge), Vercel serverless functions, and provider-internal service chaining on Fastly. Tor's `meek-azure` was retired 2024 in favor of Snowflake + WebTunnel.

### Tor pluggable transports: obfs4/Lyrebird, Snowflake, WebTunnel

**obfs4 / Lyrebird** (renamed 2023) implements Elligator2-obfuscated ntor: client computes `X' = Elligator2_inverse_map(X)` indistinguishable from uniform random bytes, sends `X' ‖ P_C(85–8128 B pad) ‖ M_C(HMAC-128) ‖ E(hours-since-epoch) ‖ MAC_C`. Server replies symmetrically; 2-hour clock skew tolerance; failed MACs induce random-delay disconnect. KDF produces 144 bytes for NaCl secretbox (XSalsa20-Poly1305) payload plus SipHash-2-4 frame-length obfuscation. **IAT modes** 0/1/2 control inter-arrival time splitting (commonly disabled now — the GFW looks at content entropy more than timing, and IAT hurts throughput). **obfs4 is detected by the GFW's FET classifier** because its stream looks fully random from byte 0 — none of the exemption rules fires. This is the existential driver behind the Tor Project's pivot to WebTunnel.

**Snowflake** is WebRTC-based: broker matches censored clients with volunteer browser proxies, a DataChannel establishes SCTP-over-DTLS-over-UDP, Tor cells flow inside. The broker is reached via domain-fronted HTTPS (primary: Fastly) with uTLS mimicry. ICE/STUN does NAT traversal; TURN rarely. Volunteer churn and the legitimacy of consumer WebRTC (Zoom, Meet, Discord) keep IP-blocking infeasible. Fingerprint concerns target **DTLS ClientHello** (the Pion default is distinguishable from browser stacks) — 2024–2025 work adds Chrome/Firefox DTLS mimicry. Russia blocked Snowflake briefly in December 2021 (DTLS extension signature) and again November 2024 (apparent traffic-analysis on DataChannel behavior); mitigations deployed each time. Snowflake is **blocked in China by default** since ~January 2024 but works intermittently via private bridges.

**WebTunnel** (launched March 12, 2024) is inspired by Frolov's HTTPT. Each bridge is a TLS reverse proxy co-hosted with a real website at the same IP:443; clients request a **secret URL path** with `Upgrade: websocket`, triggering the Tor tunnel; all other paths serve the cover site. TLS 1.3 with uTLS mimicry and a real CA-signed cert for a real domain means **WebTunnel passes the FET classifier's protocol-magic exemption** — it's real HTTPS, not random bytes. Deployment: 60 bridges at launch, 143+ by late 2024, 200 goal. Works in China and Russia; intermittently unreliable in Iran where HTTPS-to-unknown-SNI is throttled.

**Pluggable Transports 3.0** formalizes the dispatcher/API duality (in-process linking or external-binary IPC), adds typed STATUS messages, tighter error codes (EX_UNAVAILABLE=69), and CBOR-encoded args over IPC superseding the SOCKS5-auth-field kludge. Lyrebird, Shapeshifter-Dispatcher, and Swift/Java PT libraries for Onion Browser and Orbot are reference implementations.

### Decoy routing: TapDance and Conjure

**Refraction Networking** places proxy functionality at cooperating ISP border routers ("stations"). **TapDance** (Wustrow et al., USENIX Security 2014) required a real decoy TLS site whose traffic traversed the station; client injected a steganographic tag into the ClientHello, station recognized it and impersonated the decoy going forward. Production problems (TLS 1.3 breaks the injection primitive; asymmetric routing; long-lived "stuck" decoy connections as observable anomalies; BGP-level decoy-avoidance) motivated **Conjure** (Frolov et al., CCS 2019).

Conjure uses **unused/phantom IP space** within the cooperating AS: the client registers via an encrypted payload, both sides derive an upcoming phantom IP and port, the client connects to the phantom, the station (passive tap + kernel DNAT) spoofs the SYN-ACK and becomes the endpoint. IPv6 /32 gives 2^96 phantoms (enumeration infeasible); IPv4 needs per-client firewalling. The station runs **PF_RING zero-copy** on 10/40 Gbps interfaces. **Current deployment**: Merit Network, University of Michigan, and CU Boulder, continuously since January 2021. Integrated as `CONJURE-OSSH` in Psiphon's mobile clients — heavy Iranian use.

**Iran measurements** (Alaraj & Wustrow, AsiaCCS 2025, *"Proxies as Sensors"*) identified two censor types: packet-injector ASes (AS197207, AS44244, AS58224, AS57218) that RST-inject on phantom subnet traffic, and IP-blocklisting ASes. AS197207 pre-blocked subnets from the public phantom list, collaterally affecting U.S. university space. Newly allocated /24 phantoms had ~3-week detection latency.

### Psiphon, Lantern, MTProto, WARP

**Psiphon** implements per-protocol parallel tunnel establishment with multi-armed-bandit selection and runtime "tactics" parameter exchange. Core protocols in `psiphon-tunnel-core`: SSH, OSSH (obfuscated SSH — XOR + random padding), TLS-OSSH (OSSH inside uTLS TLS 1.3), SHADOWSOCKS-OSSH (layered), UNFRONTED/FRONTED-MEEK-OSSH variants, QUIC-OSSH with version modes (gQUIC 39/43/44, v1, RANDOMIZED_V1, OBFUSCATED_V1, DECOY_V1), TAPDANCE-OSSH, and CONJURE-OSSH. Scale is hundreds of millions of Android installs with heavy Iran concentration.

**Lantern** uses uTLS Chrome mimicry for outbound TLS, active-probe-resistant servers, Fastly domain fronting, and — new in 2024 — a **Kindling** bootstrapping library combining fronting, proxyless dialing, DoH tunneling, and AMP-cache rendezvous. A **WATER (WASM Transport Executables Runtime)** lets clients hot-load new pluggable transports as WASM modules without binary updates — a fast response to novel blocking.

**MTProto Proxy** carries Telegram's native MTProto 2.0 (AES-256-IGE, RSA-2048 key exchange, non-AEAD key-dependent IV). Modern secrets use `ee…` **Fake-TLS** format: client crafts a 517-byte ClientHello whose `random` is `HMAC-SHA256(secret, "ClientHello-random" ‖ timestamp)` with SNI advertising a cover domain. Server verifies the random, completes a fake TLS 1.3 session, then carries MTProto frames as ApplicationData; on mismatch it proxies to the real SNI for active-probe resistance. **Russia's TSPU on April 1, 2026** added a `TELEGRAM_TLS` signature based on the ClientHello's broken extension type `0xfe02` and 20-byte key_share (vs standard 32); client patches (Android PR #1949, iOS PR #2090) changed to `0xfe0d` / 32 bytes.

**Cloudflare WARP** is modified WireGuard (3-byte "reserved" per-client identifier) terminating on Cloudflare anycast (`162.159.192.0/24`, UDP 2408, fallback 443; iOS also offers IKEv2/IPsec). **Blocked in China** (UDP 2408 drops; GFW IP ranges). **Partially blocked in Iran** since February 2024; Cloudflare SNI-wise blocks on MCI and similar. Users chain WARP as an *inner* hop after an outer censorship-resistant tunnel via Xray's native `wireguard` outbound.

### Mobile clients, CDN tunneling, multiplexing, PQ

Mobile: **v2rayNG** (Android, Xray-core; dominant VLESS/REALITY/Vision client), **NekoBox / Nekoray** (sing-box-based; supplanted Matsuri 2022), **Shadowrocket** (iOS proprietary; dominant on iPhone). **sing-box** (SagerNet) is the unified Go implementation covering SS/SS-2022/ShadowTLS/VLESS/Vision/REALITY/VMess/Trojan/Hysteria1-2/TUIC/WireGuard/SSH/Tor — the de-facto reference core since 2023. Server panels: **3x-ui**, **Hiddify**, **Marzban** (optimized for Iran/Russia/China deployments).

**CDN tunneling** survives via Cloudflare Workers + WebSocket + VLESS: a Worker accepts WS upgrade and dials VLESS to a backend (popular repos: `zizifn/edgetunnel`, `3Kmfi6HP/CF-Workers-SUB`). Cloudflare cannot be wholesale IP-blocked by the GFW (collateral too large); CF has periodically closed loopholes via TOS and CPU throttling. **Vercel serverless** functions offer a similar H2/H3 tunneling path.

**Multiplexing** trade-offs: **Mux.Cool** (V2Ray/Xray) and **Smux** (Trojan-Go, Brook) multiplex streams on one TCP connection — reducing per-flow handshake fingerprints but creating TCP-level head-of-line blocking. QUIC-native multiplexing (NaïveProxy over H3, TUIC, Hysteria2) avoids HOL at the transport layer, making it the correct choice in high-loss censored environments. Xue et al. (USENIX 2024) specifically recommend multiplexing as the top mitigation against encapsulated-TLS-handshake fingerprinting, reducing detection by >70%.

**Post-quantum**: Xray-core v25.x supports **ML-KEM-768 + X25519 hybrid** KEM and optional **ML-DSA-65** signature for REALITY's ephemeral cert chain. This is required because Chrome has shipped `X25519MLKEM768` by default since 2024 — not mimicking it is itself a fingerprint. **Shadowsocks-2022 is already post-quantum-secure** (BLAKE3 + symmetric AEAD with pre-shared keys — no public-key primitives to harvest). Trojan/Hysteria/TUIC/NaïveProxy inherit PQ readiness from their TLS stack (BoringSSL, quic-go, Go 1.24+ added hybrid PQ February 2025).

## Part 4 — The 2024–2026 arms race

### What currently works

**China.** Community consensus (net4people, XTLS, V2Ray) on the "survive-in-China" stack is **VLESS + xtls-rprx-vision + raw/TCP + REALITY**, `fp=chrome`, dest = same-ASN HTTPS site with TLS 1.3, HTTP/2, non-redirect, OCSP stapling, encrypted-handshake after ServerHello, plus `shortIds` for client rotation. Shadowsocks-2022 survives behind ShadowTLS v3 or Cloak; Hysteria2 with Brutal and Salamander is a solid UDP fallback (though UDP timing detection on non-Brutal CCAs is a concern); NaïveProxy remains the most conservative bet on pure TLS fingerprint but inherits the universal TLS-in-TLS problem. Tor requires **WebTunnel** on obscure ASN hosting; obfs4 is detected by the FET classifier ~2 weeks after bridge publication; Snowflake works patchy on private bridges. High-traffic "airport" (机场) direct servers survive days to weeks before port-banning; the August 20, 2025 unconditional TCP/443 RST+ACK incident showed the GFW can pull the rug on 443 at will. Port blocking alternating across MCCI/ISP is frequent (net4people #306, #277).

**Iran.** VLESS+REALITY is the workhorse with severe caveats: MCCI (AS43358) began blocking REALITY within hours in August 2023 (net4people #277); MCI and MTN have divergent blocklists requiring multi-endpoint fallback (net4people #253); MCCI uses a graylist/whitelist for VPS hosting IPs (Breaking Nasdaq, 2025). Cloudflare fronting is mostly dead; Arvan Cloud fronting suppressed. Mmmray demonstrated in May 2024 that Iranian VPN vendors abuse REALITY servers as SNI-proxies to Cloudflare Workers (net4people #359), with UUIDs extracted from decrypted inner traffic matching public GitHub proxy-list repos. Hysteria2 is theoretically ideal for Iran's lossy networks — Brutal CCA is designed exactly for this — but UDP is often blocked, throttled, or has 50% packet loss (net4people #182). During the 2025–2026 whitelist phases only DNS/HTTP/HTTPS traverse the TIC chokepoint, so every UDP-based protocol fails. **Psiphon, with HTTPS-tunneled transports, and Starlink (now a capital offense) are the residual lifelines.**

### What has been recently blocked or detected

- **GFW QUIC SNI censorship** (April 7, 2024 — Zohaib et al. USENIX 2025). Mitigations: QUIC v2, SNI-slicing across CRYPTO frames (quic-go v0.52.0, May 2025), Chrome Chaos Protection, ML-KEM-oversized ClientHellos.
- **GFW TCP/443 unconditional RST+ACK** (August 20, 2025, 74 minutes; GFW Report).
- **Henan provincial firewall** (IEEE S&P 2025; 4.2M cumulative domains, stateless, bypassable via TCP segmentation and TLS record fragmentation).
- **Wallbleed disclosure** (NDSS 2025, partial patch November 2023, full March 2024).
- **Geedge/MESA 500 GB leak** (September 11, 2025; export of GFW platform to Myanmar, Pakistan, Ethiopia, Kazakhstan under Belt and Road).
- **TrojanProbe** implementation-specific probe distinguishers (Feng et al. 2024).
- **Xue et al. encapsulated-TLS-handshake fingerprinting** (USENIX 2024; >70% TPR / 0.054% FPR).
- **Cross-layer RTT fingerprinting** (NDSS 2025).
- **Iran IPv6 block** (May 2024).
- **Iran DPI equipment**: Sandvine sanctioned February 2024; Protei (Russian) deployed at Ariantel and likely across mobile carriers; Geedge leak suggests Chinese platforms diffusing.

### REALITY vulnerabilities, correctly scoped

No peer-reviewed paper claims a cryptographic MITM break of REALITY. The extant attacks are **traffic-analysis**, not protocol-level: Xue et al.'s TLS-in-TLS fingerprinting (works against REALITY+VLESS+Vision), cross-layer RTT skew, IP clustering via X25519 public-key reuse, stolen-cert ASN mismatches, fallback-rate-limiting fingerprints, and stolen-cert size mismatches after Chrome's X25519MLKEM768 rollout. The community guidance (XTLS README, ObjShadow 2024, net4people #528) is clear: REALITY raises the cost of blocking; it does not confer undetectability.

### Iran's trajectory

Iran is converging on **identity-bound, class-based, default-deny Internet**: the February 2024 National Portal for Iranians binds every user from birth to SIM/IP/traffic class; the February 2024 SCC Statute criminalizes unlicensed VPN use; the July 2025 bylaw formalizes tiered access; the late-2025 Anti-Espionage Law criminalizes Starlink possession up to the death penalty; the November 2025 User Protection Bill re-submission legally binds all executive bodies to SNSC's internet-disconnect mechanism. Combined with protocol whitelisting at TIC and Protei/Geedge-class DPI, this is the most advanced non-China implementation of an identity-gated national Internet.

## Where the arms race is heading

The cat-and-mouse has moved into three new layers. **First, in-path UDP enforcement** — the GFW's April 2024 QUIC SNI blocking ended a decade of UDP immunity and shows the state has gained inline capability at least for targeted flows. **Second, encapsulated-TLS-handshake fingerprinting** — Xue et al.'s protocol-agnostic classifier threatens every TLS-wrapped proxy at once, and the only documented mitigation (multiplexing) trades per-flow detection for coarser fingerprints. **Third, infrastructure denial instead of content detection** — Iran's protocol whitelist and identity-gated NIN renders elaborate fingerprint evasion moot when default policy drops the packet. Circumvention is responding with **probe-resistant real-site impersonation (REALITY, WebTunnel, Trojan-style fallback), auto-generated novel protocols (UPGen/Proteus, WATER-hot-loaded WASM transports), ISP-level decoy routing (Conjure, now in Psiphon), and PQ-hybrid TLS to stay inside the Chrome fingerprint envelope.**

The defensive edge now belongs to tools that look *exactly* like an allowed real application on the wire — not to tools that look random. The offensive edge increasingly belongs to states that replace content inspection with infrastructure control: whitelists, identity binding, and the political power to blackhole entire CDN ranges irrespective of collateral damage. The Iran January 2026 blackout, 53 days and counting, suggests a regime willing to accept $35 million a day in direct costs to maintain isolation. Against that determination, technical circumvention remains necessary but no longer sufficient.
